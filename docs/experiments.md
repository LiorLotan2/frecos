# Experiments

This document covers the four experiments run against the FreCoS extension: the
bracketing comparison (A8, plus its calibration-sweep follow-up), the ablation (A9),
and the three sweeps (A10). Every number below comes from the committed
`results/*/results.csv` files or their accompanying `summary.md`; nothing here is
recomputed independently of those. Figures are generated by `analysis/make_figures.py`
and live in `analysis/figures/`; see that directory's README note in `analysis/` for
the one-command regeneration path.

All four experiments share one limitation worth stating up front rather than repeating
per section: the benchmark harness currently uses an exact-text-match index, not a real
semantic embedding search. Only literal repeats of a canonical query string can hit;
paraphrases never do. This caps hit counts across every run (roughly 100-350 hits out
of 7,500-12,600 scored queries, depending on the experiment) and widens every confidence
interval accordingly. It does not obviously favor one policy or lambda source over
another, since it applies identically across all conditions in every experiment, but it
does mean the absolute hit-rate and stale-hit-rate numbers below are not representative
of what a semantic index would produce, and the report needs to say so plainly.

## Bracketing (global vs. learned vs. oracle lambda)

W1 eval split, gate enabled, FreCoS eviction, cache size 1650 entries (25% of the
6600-answer_id working set), ttl_confidence 0.9, cluster_count_k 10. Three
`lambda_source` values, 10 seeds each, 30 rows total in `results/brackets/results.csv`.

Learned lambda separates cleanly from global: median stale_hit_rate 0.0350 for learned
against 0.1436 for global, 95% CIs (0.0242, 0.0604) and (0.0851, 0.2143) respectively,
non-overlapping, Mann-Whitney U p ~ 0.0005. Global is significantly worse.

Where this experiment departs from what the design anticipated: learned does not land
strictly between global and oracle. Its median stale_hit_rate (0.0350) equals oracle's
median exactly, 8 of 10 seeds are byte-identical between the two conditions, and
Mann-Whitney U on learned vs. oracle gives p ~ 0.94, no detectable difference. Learned
sits at the oracle end of the bracket, not at a visible midpoint. The reason is not
mysterious: at 550-650 calibration observations per cluster, well above the fitter's
n_obs >= 30 fallback floor, the per-cluster MLE essentially recovers the true generator
lambda, so there is no daylight left for "learned" and "oracle" to differ on.

A follow-up run (`results/brackets/calibration_sweep/`) tested whether that gap opens up
under a 6.7x smaller trace (1800 queries instead of 12000), which drops per-cluster
calibration counts to roughly 40-70 observations, about an order of magnitude sparser
than the original run. It doesn't. Learned vs. oracle is still indistinguishable
(p ~ 0.96), while learned vs. global remains significant (p ~ 0.0015, medians 0.0000 vs.
0.1742). The reason the gap still doesn't open is that shrinking the trace shrinks the
eval-split hit count in lockstep with the calibration sample, and it's the eval-side hit
count that would need to be large for calibration noise to show up in stale_hit_rate;
with single-digit-to-thirties hit counts per run, the ratio is too coarse to register the
noisier lambda estimate.

So the finding this experiment actually establishes, stated plainly rather than dressed
up as the originally-expected "strictly between" result: learned lambda beats a single
pooled global rate by a large and significant margin, and tracks the oracle rather than
sitting partway to it, robustly across a roughly tenfold range of calibration sample
sizes. That is a stronger form of the underlying claim (per-cluster fitting beats
pooling) than the design doc's own success criterion asked for, even though the literal
bracket shape it anticipated didn't materialize. Cost_saved_usd is flat across all three
lambda sources in both runs, for a mechanical reason common to every experiment here:
cost_saved only counts non-stale hits, and with the exact-match index the hit set itself
barely changes across conditions, so lambda_source mostly redistributes hits between
"served fresh" and "served stale" rather than changing which queries hit at all.

Figure: `analysis/figures/fig2_brackets.png` shows both runs on one axis (the original
12000-query bracket as solid points, the sparser 1800-query follow-up as dashed points
alongside it), which is the clearest way to show the same shape holding at both scales.

## Ablation (gate, eviction, size normalization)

W1 eval split, 6 rows x 10 seeds = 60 rows in `results/ablation/results.csv`, same trace
generation parameters as the bracket experiment. Six rows: (1) LRU with the gate off,
stock GPTCache behavior; (2) LFU with the gate off, the actual floor per Biton &
Friedman's own finding that LRU is weak on semantic workloads; (3) a documented
LFU-with-cost-tiebreak substitute for Biton & Friedman's released policy (their code
wasn't reachable in this build environment); (4) TTLGate on top of LFU, isolating the
gate; (5) TTLGate plus full FreCoS eviction, the full stack; (6) the same full stack with
the `/size_bytes` term dropped.

Row 2 is the comparison of record, not row 1 - grading against LRU alone would flatter
every other row trivially. Against that floor (median stale_hit_rate 0.6798), the gate
rows collapse stale_hit_rate by roughly 15x: row 4 (gate+LFU) reaches 0.0420, row 5
(gate+FreCoS) reaches 0.0350, row 6 (no-size) reaches 0.0421. Hit_rate drops by about a
third in the same rows (0.0518 down to ~0.017), which is the trade-off the design
predicted: the gate converts stale hits into misses by construction.

Row 3 turned out to be identical to row 2 in every correctness and cost column, seed by
seed. This isn't a copy-paste bug; at this hit rate almost every entry at eviction time
has freq=0, so the substitute's freq/regen_cost score is 0 for essentially every
candidate and the policy falls through to the same tie-break LFU uses. That means the
primary-comparator slot is effectively untested by this particular workload's
parameters, a limitation worth stating rather than hiding behind a coincidence-shaped
result.

The interaction check the design flagged in advance - does the gate carry most of the
stale-hit-rate improvement, with FreCoS's decay term contributing less on top of it than
it would alone - held, and held more strongly than anticipated. Gate alone (row 4 vs.
row 2): delta -0.6379. FreCoS's decay term on top of the gate (row 5 vs. row 4): delta
-0.0070, not statistically distinguishable from zero (Mann-Whitney U p ~ 0.97). The gate
accounts for about 99% of the total improvement from floor to full stack. The
size-normalization row (6) is likewise statistically indistinguishable from row 5 and
row 4 on every metric; the size term doesn't move the needle at this cache size,
plausibly because eviction pressure among gate-protected, already-fresh entries doesn't
differentiate much on any of FreCoS's four terms once staleness itself is off the table.
The cache-size sweep, run at tighter budgets, is a better place to look for the size
term's effect than this ablation. Cost_saved_usd sits in the 0.44-0.45 range across all
six rows for the same exact-match-index reason noted above: the hit set barely changes
across policies, so cost savings barely move even though which hits are stale changes a
great deal.

Figure: `analysis/figures/fig1_ablation_stale_hit_rate.png`, the headline figure, bars
with 95% CIs for all six rows, row 2 marked as the floor and its level drawn as a
reference line across the chart.

## Cache-size sweep

W1, gate enabled, FreCoS eviction, learned lambda. Five cache-size points at 5%, 15%,
30%, 50%, 80% of the 6600-entry working set (330, 990, 1980, 3300, 5280 entries), 10
seeds each, 50 rows in `results/sweeps/cache_size/results.csv`.

Hit_rate rises from 0.0130 at 330 entries to 0.0174 at 1980 entries, then flattens
completely: 1980, 3300, and 5280 entries produce identical per-seed hit counts,
query-by-query. Stale_hit_rate follows the same increasing-then-flat shape, since it's a
ratio computed over the same fixed hit set once hit_rate stops moving. The knee is
identified by marginal slope (delta hit_rate / delta entries) between consecutive
points: the slope drops by roughly 13x going into the 990-to-1980 step and by a further
~1000x, to exactly zero, going into the 1980-to-3300 step. 1980 entries (30% of the
working set) is therefore the knee - the last point where growing the cache still bought
any measurable hit-rate improvement at all. This is the anchor point used elsewhere
(the bracket and ablation experiments both fix cache size near this range).

As with every other experiment here, this saturation point is a function of the
exact-match index: with only literal repeats able to hit, the hit set is small and
saturates fast. A real semantic index would likely push the knee out to a larger cache
size, since paraphrase traffic that currently never hits would start contending for
cache slots.

Figure: `analysis/figures/fig4_cache_size_knee.png`, hit rate against cache size with
the knee marked at 1980 entries.

## TTL-confidence sweep

W1, gate enabled, FreCoS eviction, learned lambda, cache size fixed at 1980 (the knee
identified above), cluster_count_k 10. Four confidence points (0.8, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99), 10
seeds each, 40 rows in `results/sweeps/ttl_confidence/results.csv`.

This is the honest core of the project: the gate lowers hit rate by construction, so the
question is the shape of the exchange rate, not whether either metric wins outright.
Both metrics move monotonically as confidence rises, with no reversals across the four
points. Stale_hit_rate falls from 0.1263 at confidence 0.8 to 0.0079 at confidence 0.99,
a 16x reduction. Hit_rate falls from 0.0192 to 0.0163 over the same range, a 15%
reduction. The exchange rate is not uniform across the range: most of the
stale-hit-rate benefit is captured in the first step, from 0.80 to 0.90 (0.1263 down to
0.0420), while hit_rate erodes at a comparatively steady pace across the whole range
(0.0192, 0.0174, 0.0170, 0.0163). In other words, the cheapest gains in correctness come
early; pushing confidence toward 0.99 buys diminishing stale-hit-rate reduction for
roughly the same ongoing hit-rate cost per step.

This is presented here, and should be presented in the report, as a trade-off curve,
not a claim that a stricter gate is uniformly better - a system operator choosing a
confidence level is choosing a point on this curve, not looking for a dominant option.

Figure: `analysis/figures/fig3_ttl_confidence_tradeoff.png`, stale_hit_rate and hit_rate
both plotted against ttl_confidence on twin axes, so the relative steepness of the two
curves is visible directly.

## Cluster-count-K sweep

W1, gate enabled, FreCoS eviction, learned lambda, cache size 1980, ttl_confidence 0.9.
Four K points (5, 10, 20, 50), 10 seeds each, 40 rows in
`results/sweeps/cluster_k/results.csv`.

This is the one axis that came out non-monotone: hit_rate goes 0.0183, 0.0174, 0.0164,
0.0178 as K goes 5, 10, 20, 50, dipping through the middle and partially recovering at
K=50; stale_hit_rate goes 0.0326, 0.0420, 0.0477, 0.0341, rising then falling. The 95%
CIs overlap heavily at every adjacent pair (for example hit_rate at K=10 and K=20:
(0.0171, 0.0180) vs. (0.0156, 0.0180)), so none of these differences are distinguishable
from noise at 10 seeds per point. A plausible mechanism, noted in the sweep's own
summary: increasing K spreads the same fixed query volume over more, smaller per-cluster
calibration samples, making the learned lambda per cluster noisier, while at the same
time finer-grained clusters could in principle help - two effects pulling in opposite
directions that appear to roughly cancel at this trace size.

Because this result has neither a large effect size nor a tight CI, it is not promoted
to a headline figure, per the analysis mandate to keep only the 3-4 tightest, largest
results as plots. Its summary statistics (median hit_rate and stale_hit_rate with 95%
CIs at each K) are recorded in `analysis/figures/supplementary.csv` instead. The honest
conclusion, and the one worth keeping in the report's discussion, is that cluster count
does not have a detectable monotone effect on either metric at this workload scale; a
larger trace or more seeds would be needed to say anything sharper about its direction.
