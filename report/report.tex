\begin{abstract}
Semantic caches are evaluated on hit rate, latency, and at best false-hit rate. None of
these distinguishes a correct hit from a stale one. I name and measure that gap directly:
\emph{stale-hit-rate}, the fraction of served cache hits whose content was no longer valid
at serve time. I extend GPTCache, a frozen and widely used open-source semantic cache,
with a per-cluster staleness model fit from observed data and consumed in two places: a
validity gate on the serve path, and a soft decay term in eviction. Across a bracketing
experiment, a six-row ablation, three parameter sweeps, and two model-misspecification
checks, all run with ten seeds per configuration, the gate cuts stale-hit-rate roughly
19-fold against a frequency-based floor while removing only about 3\% of useful hits, and
useful-hit-rate stays flat across most of the confidence range I swept. A per-cluster
learned decay rate beats a single pooled rate by a wide, significant margin, and continues
to when the workload is easier to predict than the fitter assumes; a second, adversarial
misspecification where fitting is genuinely harder is reported too. I measure the trade-off
at the level of individual queries, not only hit-rate percentages, and I report which of
the two mechanisms, the gate or the eviction term, is doing the correctness work.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}

A semantic cache sits in front of an expensive backend, usually a large language model
call. It serves a stored response when an incoming query is close enough in embedding
space to one it has already answered. The promise is simple: skip the call, return the
same answer faster and for free. But ``the same answer'' is a claim about time as well as
similarity, and most semantic caches never check the time part.

The gap matters most in systems that place a language model between a user and an answer
about content that changes on its own schedule, such as monitoring how a brand is
represented across AI-generated answers (the workload behind answer engine optimization, or
AEO). These systems ask the same style of question repeatedly, exactly the shape of
workload a semantic cache absorbs, but the facts being asked about do not sit still: a
product goes out of stock, a price changes, a review's sentiment shifts. A cache that asks
only ``have I seen a similar question before'' and never ``is that answer still true'' will
serve a stale answer indefinitely once written. The system on top of it cannot tell a
correct hit from a wrong one, because the metric that would, stale-hit-rate, does not exist
in standard cache evaluation.

Two concrete gaps follow, and motivate the two components I add.

\textbf{No staleness concept in the serve path.} A cached answer about a fact that changes
on its own schedule is served indefinitely once written. ``Found in cache'' is silently
treated as ``correct,'' with no mechanism to reconsider that judgment.

\textbf{No cost term in eviction.} Count-based policies such as LRU and LFU treat two
entries with identical access patterns identically, even when one is far more expensive to
regenerate. A policy blind to regeneration cost evicts a rarely accessed but expensive
entry ahead of a cheap one accessed once more recently.

Industry and research reflect this gap unevenly. Frontier model providers do exact-prefix
key-value caching with a wall-clock time-to-live and no similarity matching. Semantic-caching
products use a single fixed global similarity threshold with plain time-to-live or
count-based eviction. Neither has a notion of per-topic staleness. The research literature
is further ahead, proposing cost-aware and staleness-aware eviction formulas, but none of it
names or measures stale-hit-rate as an evaluation metric. I compare against that literature,
not the weaker product baseline.

This report makes four contributions: (1) stale-hit-rate as a named, measured evaluation
metric; (2) a per-cluster staleness model, fit from data, consumed as both a serving-time
validity gate and an eviction-time decay term; (3) an ablation that isolates which of the
two consumers does the correctness work; and (4) a misspecification check that tests
whether the staleness model's own assumption, that content validity decays exponentially,
matters to the result. Section~2 justifies the baseline, Section~3 describes the design,
Section~4 the workload and harness, and Section~5 reports five experiments.

\subsection{Related work}

The closest prior art, and my primary eviction comparator, is Biton and Friedman's work on
semantic-cache eviction~[1]. It proves optimal offline semantic-cache eviction is NP-hard,
supplies polynomial-time heuristics, and presents online policies combining recency,
frequency, and locality. It evaluates these on GPTCache and releases its implementation,
part of why I chose GPTCache as the baseline here. Two of its findings shape my design
directly: LRU is a weak policy on semantic workloads, and frequency-based policies are a
much stronger baseline. That is why every comparison here treats an LFU-class policy, not
LRU, as the floor. The delta against FreCoS (\textbf{Fre}shness- and \textbf{Co}st-aware
\textbf{S}erving, this project's name): their policies combine access-pattern signals only,
while FreCoS adds two signals orthogonal to access pattern, regeneration cost and content
validity over time, and evaluates against a correctness metric, stale-hit-rate, their
setting does not define.

The structurally closest published eviction formula is Cortex's LCFU policy~[2], whose
earlier preprint circulated under the name Asteria and is cited here by its current title.
LCFU scores an entry as a product of log-scaled frequency, cost, latency, and a staleness
term, divided by size, with a time-to-live purge. The delta is narrow: Cortex's staleness
term is a static ``staticity'' score assigned by a language model at write time, while
FreCoS's is a decay rate learned per cluster from observed staleness. The distinction is
learned against assigned, and the bracketing experiment in Section~\ref{sec:brackets} tests
whether that learning does measurable work. I did not reimplement Cortex's formula, since it
incorporates remote-tool-call latency metadata with no equivalent here.

Several other lines of work bear on individual pieces of the design without matching its
combination. GDSF~[3] scores entries as cost times frequency divided by size with a
global-clock aging term, but that term ages access recency, not content validity, so it has
no freshness semantics. Category-Aware caching~[4] assigns per-category time-to-live values
that are load-based rather than learned, and defines no stale-hit-rate equivalent. It is
also why I cut a third, originally planned component, calibrated per-cluster similarity
thresholds: it would have been a lossy special case of both this work and per-embedding
threshold learning. FreshCache~[5] shares FreCoS's lineage of putting staleness in the
serve path, but as a probabilistic risk budget per tier rather than a hard gate; I list it
as future work as an alternative framing. SCALM~[6] clusters cache entries similarly to my
k-means step, but its significance score is not staleness-aware. MeanCache~[7] is
cost-motivated but does not target eviction. W-TinyLFU~[8] is an admission-control policy,
deciding whether an entry is worth admitting before eviction is invoked; it is out of
scope here since it would add an orthogonal axis rather than test either of my
contributions.

I considered and rejected the alternative baseline vCache~[9]. Its contribution is
per-prompt threshold learning, replacing a single global threshold with one learned per
embedding, and eviction is explicitly not its focus. Building this staleness and cost work
on top of vCache would layer a new contribution on a system whose own novelty sits
elsewhere, diluting rather than sharpening the comparison.

\section{Baseline}

\subsection{Why GPTCache}

I extend GPTCache~[10] rather than a more actively maintained semantic cache. The case rests
on architecture and control properties, not community activity. GPTCache's most recent
release is v0.1.44 (August 2024); it carries dozens of open issues and pull requests, and
the maintainers state that support for new integrations has stopped. Ordinarily this counts
against a dependency. Here it is an advantage: a frozen interface is a better scientific
control than a moving one. The baseline I measured early in this project is byte-identical
to the one I measured at the end, so every delta reported below is attributable to the
extension, not an upstream change mid-project.

The second reason is architectural fit, which I verified by reading the vendored source
directly rather than assuming it from documentation. Every component I modify, the
similarity threshold check, the hit and miss decision, and the eviction registry, sits
behind a registered, named interface. My extension is additive configuration: a new
eviction subclass registered through the existing hook, a metadata field added via
composition around storage, and a serving-path gate plugged into one shared decision
helper. I never edit vendored code. Every claim about GPTCache's internal behavior quoted
here was re-verified against the exact pinned commit vendored in this project's repository,
with file and line recorded.

Third, GPTCache is the baseline the closest prior art already uses. In principle this makes
Biton and Friedman's released policy usable directly as my primary comparator; in practice
I substituted a documented proxy instead, for reasons stated in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}.
Fourth, its default embedding backend runs on the CPU deterministically, keeping every
benchmark run reproducible without a GPU dependency or nondeterminism from a hosted
embedding service.

One consequence of this choice: the course guidelines offer a grade bonus for an upstream
contribution adopted by maintainers, and GPTCache's own README states that new
contributions are not being merged. Choosing GPTCache for the reproducibility argument
above forecloses that bonus, a trade I made deliberately.

\subsection{GPTCache's behavior and default eviction policy}

GPTCache serves a cached response when an incoming query is semantically close enough to a
previously seen one, using a single global cosine similarity threshold, 0.8 by default,
rather than exact-match keys. Its eviction policies are purely count-based:
least-recently-used, least-frequently-used, first-in-first-out, or random replacement. None
reads generation time, access recency, or regeneration cost beyond the single counter each
already tracks. None retains metadata on an evicted entry; the eviction cache stores only a
boolean flag per key. Creation and last-access timestamps are persisted per entry but never
read by any decision logic. They exist as columns with no downstream consumer, precisely
the gap my extension repurposes. There is no admission control; every miss is written back
unconditionally. No cost accounting, false-hit tracking, or staleness tracking exists
anywhere in the built-in reporting layer, which counts only per-stage operation timers.

\section{Design}

\subsection{Contribution}

The contribution is one learned artifact with two consumers. FreCoS fits a per-cluster
staleness model and uses it at both ends of the cache's lifecycle: as a hard validity gate
when serving a candidate hit, and as a soft decay term when choosing an eviction victim.
The metric that makes both uses visible is stale-hit-rate: the fraction of served hits
whose content was already invalid when served.

Cluster assignment is a fixed, seeded k-means clustering over cached-query embeddings, run
once at cache-build time, with no online re-clustering, a deliberate reproducibility
choice. I compute these embeddings because the staleness model needs cluster identity;
Section~\ref{ssec:simmodel} explains why the benchmark harness used to evaluate the model
does not also use them for cache lookup. Cluster identity, once assigned, is consumed by
both the gate and the eviction policy and never recomputed independently by either. A gate
and an eviction policy that disagree about an entry's cluster is one of the most likely
integration failures in a system with this shape, so I checked for it directly in the
ablation (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

For each cluster, a decay rate $\lambda_c$ is fit from observed staleness in a held-out
calibration split, assuming validity decays exponentially: $\Pr[\text{still valid after }
\Delta t] = e^{-\lambda_c \Delta t}$. A time-to-live is derived at a configured confidence
level $c$ as $\text{ttl} = -\ln(c) / \lambda_c$. Three interchangeable modes produce this
table with the same output shape: \emph{learned} fits each cluster's rate independently by
maximum likelihood, falling back to a rate pooled across all clusters below thirty
observations; \emph{global} pools every cluster into one shared rate regardless of sample
size; \emph{oracle} takes the rate from the workload generator's ground truth, an upper
bound never available in a real deployment. Table~\ref{tab:params} lists every tunable
parameter, its default, and where its effect is measured.

The validity gate is the first consumer. On a candidate hit, if the entry's age exceeds
its cluster's time-to-live, it is treated as a miss, the answer is regenerated, and the
event counts as a stale hit prevented rather than served. This is a single comparison
against creation time; the gate never reads last-access time, since staleness is a
property of when an answer was generated, not how recently it was requested, and a popular
but outdated entry must not be treated as fresh simply because it is popular.

The eviction value function is the second consumer:

\begin{equation}
\text{value} = \log(2 + \text{freq}) \cdot \log(1 + \kappa \cdot \text{regen\_cost}) \cdot e^{-\lambda_c \cdot \text{age}} \; / \; \text{size\_bytes}
\label{eq:value}
\end{equation}

where $\text{age}$ is measured from creation time, never last access; $\kappa = 1000$
log-compresses regeneration cost (typically a few tenths of a US cent) so its heavy-tailed
distribution cannot dominate the other terms; and the victim is the entry with the lowest
value, ties broken by oldest creation time then lowest entry identifier.

One detail reflects a real design tension I ran into. My original frequency term was
$\log(1+\text{freq})$, exactly zero for a never-accessed entry. Since the value function is
a product of four terms, a zero frequency term zeroes the entire score regardless of cost,
freshness, or size: a freshly written entry would always be evicted next. A property test
against exactly this claim, described in Appendix A, failed against the literal formula,
and I fixed it by changing the term to $\log(2+\text{freq})$ in Equation~\ref{eq:value},
which preserves the same concave shape while guaranteeing a strictly positive score at zero
frequency. I applied the fix throughout every experiment in this report.

Because the gate already refreshes anything past its time-to-live, the decay term inside
the value function is not itself performing staleness prevention. It acts as a soft prior
favoring entries with more remaining useful life among candidates the gate already judged
fresh. This is the intended division of labor, and the ablation in
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} tests it directly.

Eviction remains entry-count budgeted, matching GPTCache's convention. Entry size in bytes
is recorded and used only inside the value function's denominator; I did not build a
separate byte-budget eviction loop.

An earlier design included a third component, calibrated per-cluster similarity
thresholds, which I cut before implementation began. It turns out to be a lossy special
case of two things already in the literature, vCache's per-embedding threshold learning
and Category-Aware caching's per-category treatment, so building a third, weaker version
would have cost more than it contributed. It remains future work.

\section{Experimental setup}

\subsection{Workload}

All experiments run on W1, a deterministic, seeded synthetic workload generator I built
for this project. I investigated a second workload, derived from Wikipedia revision history
with staleness ground truth from edit timestamps, in a timeboxed feasibility spike before
other implementation work began. It sampled thirty question-answer pairs from
SQuAD~2.0~[11] and compared an automatic sentence-change rule against twenty hand-labeled
revision pairs; the rule agreed with my labels only 40\% of the time, short of the 80\%
threshold I had set as a go/no-go bar, and reaching a reliable rule was estimated at
eighteen to twenty-three more hours I did not have. I proceeded with W1 alone and did not
revisit that call. The full spike, including the failure mode behind the disagreements, is
in \texttt{docs/w2-feasibility.md}. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses what this limits.

W1 generates queries across several streams inside each topic cluster: canonical queries
establishing a ground-truth answer, near-duplicate paraphrases sharing that answer's
identity, novel long-tail queries that never repeat, and time-shifted repeats of an
existing answer. Every cluster carries its own randomly drawn half-life and
regeneration-cost distribution. The generator guarantees that for every repeating answer
identity, at least one later occurrence lands past that answer's true expiry, since
otherwise the validity gate would have nothing to reject. Each trace is split by arrival
time into the first thirty percent for calibration and the remaining seventy percent for
evaluation; no parameter is fit on data it is later scored against.

The default generator draws half-life from an exponential distribution, matching the
staleness fitter's own assumption exactly. Section~\ref{ssec:misspec} reruns the bracketing
experiment with half-life drawn from a Weibull distribution instead, same mean, different
shape, to test what happens when that assumption is wrong.

The generator's two ground-truth distributions, regeneration cost and content half-life,
were meant to be calibrated against the Azure LLM Inference Trace and observed
update-frequency in a public corpus. Neither was reachable from my development
environment, so I instead parameterized both from commonly cited public figures as a
plausibility check, not a real external fit, a second limit on external validity.

\subsection{Simulation model}
\label{ssec:simmodel}

The benchmark harness every experiment runs through is a pure trace replayer; no language
model is ever called. On a miss, response content is whatever answer identity the trace
recorded, cost charged is the trace's recorded regeneration cost, and miss latency is
simulated from a seeded log-normal distribution scaled by entry size, deterministic per
query. This distribution is a documented placeholder, not fit to a real inference trace.
Hit latency and the extension's own overhead, cluster lookup plus gate check, are measured
for real with wall-clock timing: median overhead is 0.57 microseconds per decision at the
LFU floor and 0.69 microseconds at the full stack (Table~\ref{tab:latency}), what the gate
and cluster lookup add on top of GPTCache's own decision path. Reported latency in every
table is therefore a hybrid of real cache-side measurement and simulated backend cost, the
right trade for keeping every run free and deterministic, but a limitation worth stating.

A further limitation shapes every absolute number below: the harness's lookup index is
exact-text-match; I compute embeddings for clustering (Section~3.1) but not for retrieval,
so no semantic index is wired in yet. Paraphrase queries, one of W1's four stream types,
never register as hits; only literal repeats do. Hit counts across every experiment are in
the low hundreds out of thousands of scored queries (exact per-seed counts appear beside
every rate in Section~5), which widens every confidence interval and also means
false-hit-rate is exactly zero in every run of every experiment in this report, since exact
matching always retrieves the entry actually written for that string. It carries no signal
under this harness, which is why it does not appear as a column in the results
tables below; a real semantic index is the place to measure it.

The invariance I rely on for every relative comparison, that this limitation does not
favor one policy or lambda source over another, holds within each comparison because the
index is held fixed across every condition inside it. It would not necessarily survive a
swap to a semantic index: a paraphrase match would inherit an older entry's age rather than
a freshly-written one's, changing the age distribution the gate sees, and I have not
verified that the gate's or eviction policy's relative ranking survives that change.

The cache starts empty in every run. The first ten percent of the evaluation split warms
the cache and is excluded from every metric; only the remaining ninety percent is scored.

\subsection{Metrics and statistics}

Stale-hit-rate is the fraction of served hits where serve time exceeds the entry's true
expiry; false-hit-rate is the fraction where the served answer's identity does not match
the query's true identity; useful-hit-rate is the fraction of \emph{all scored queries}
(not just hits) served a correct, non-stale answer. Cost saved sums regeneration cost over
non-stale hits only, since a stale hit served a wrong answer and saved nothing. Every
configuration ran ten independent seeds, each its own freshly generated trace, since the
pipeline has no randomness once a trace is fixed. Results are medians with 95\% confidence
intervals from a percentile bootstrap over the ten per-seed values (10,000 resamples, fixed
resampling seed). With only ten seeds a bootstrap CI is coarser than it looks, so I report
raw hit and stale-hit counts alongside every rate. Group comparisons use the Mann-Whitney U
test with the rank-biserial correlation as an effect size, since at $n=10$ the smallest
achievable two-sided $p$-value is about 0.0002 and $p$ alone does not distinguish a large
effect from a small one at that floor. I ran roughly fifteen such tests without correcting
for multiple comparisons; treat borderline $p$-values with that in mind. A non-significant
result is reported as an absence of detected difference at $n=10$, not as equivalence;
where I claim two conditions behave alike, I show matching raw counts, not only a large
$p$-value.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Bracketing: does learned staleness beat naive pooling}
\label{sec:brackets}

This experiment tests the core claim behind fitting a staleness rate per cluster at all:
does it do work relative to two obvious alternatives, a rate pooled across every cluster,
and the true rate a real system could never observe. I ran three lambda sources, global,
learned, and oracle, for 10 seeds each on the same 12,000-query W1 configuration, gate
enabled, FreCoS eviction active throughout.

Learned separates cleanly from global: median stale-hit-rate 0.035 under learned against
0.144 under global, confidence intervals not overlapping, Mann-Whitney $U = 4.0$,
$p \approx 0.0005$, rank-biserial $r = 0.92$, a large effect. Pooling every cluster's
observations into one rate is significantly worse than fitting each cluster's own.

Learned and oracle are statistically indistinguishable ($U = 49.0$, $p \approx 0.94$,
$r = 0.02$): learned's median stale-hit-rate equals oracle's exactly, and eight of ten seed
pairs are byte-identical in raw stale-hit count. This is close to guaranteed by
construction, and I want to be explicit about why rather than present it as a surprise. W1
draws each cluster's true half-life from an exponential distribution; the staleness fitter
assumes exactly that distribution when it fits $\lambda_c$. At 550--650 calibration
observations per cluster here, well above the 30-observation fallback, maximum-likelihood
estimation of an exponential rate recovers the true rate almost exactly when the data
really is exponential with that rate. The defensible claim from this run alone is narrow:
if content half-lives vary by topic and are close to exponential, per-cluster fitting from
a few hundred observations recovers them and a pooled rate does not. It is not yet evidence
that learned estimation is robust when its own model is wrong; Section~\ref{ssec:misspec}
tests that directly.

A first follow-up asked whether learned and oracle separate at a substantially smaller
calibration sample, roughly sevenfold sparser (30--70 observations per cluster instead of
550--650). They do not ($p \approx 0.96$), while learned versus global remains significant
($p \approx 0.0015$). This traces to a confound, not a property of the fit: shrinking the
trace shrinks the calibration sample and the evaluation-split hit count together, and it is
the eval-side hit count, not calibration sample size alone, that determines whether
estimation noise ever surfaces in a measured stale-hit-rate. Figure~\ref{fig:brackets}
shows both runs on one axis.

\subsubsection{Misspecification: what happens when the exponential assumption is wrong}
\label{ssec:misspec}

My first attempt at this check drew W1's true half-life from a Weibull distribution
instead of an exponential, same mean per cluster, shape parameter 2 instead of the
exponential's implicit 1. This was underpowered by construction, not just in outcome: a
Weibull with shape 2 has roughly half the coefficient of variation of the exponential it
replaces, so that change made the workload's staleness \emph{easier} to predict, not
harder. Every arm's stale-hit-rate improved (global 0.144 to 0.065, learned and oracle both
to 0.000), and learned and oracle floored at exactly zero rather than visibly separating, a
floor effect from low hit counts, not a demonstration that either estimator handled
misspecification well. I report this attempt because it taught me the right axis to vary,
not because it is informative on its own.

A genuinely adversarial case needs the exponential's own maximum-likelihood fit to be
systematically biased, not merely non-exponential with a smaller spread. I reran the
bracket with each cluster's true half-life drawn from a 50/50 mixture of two exponentials,
one at a fifth of the cluster's mean scale and one at 1.8 times it, same overall mean as
before. A single fitted rate cannot be correct for both halves of a bimodal population at
once, so this should be, and is, harder for the fitter than the exponential case.

Learned versus global remains significant, more sharply than in the well-specified run
($U = 4.0$, $p \approx 0.0005$, $r = 0.92$). Learned versus oracle is, for the first time
in this report, a comparison with a real, non-degenerate gap to measure: median
stale-hit-rate 0.080 against 0.074, and this is not distinguishable from noise at $n=10$
($U = 59.0$, $p \approx 0.50$, $r = 0.18$, a small-to-moderate effect not confirmed
significant). Learned is close to oracle but no longer identical to it seed by seed the way
it was under a matched or an easier-than-matched model, which is consistent with the
mixture being a genuinely harder case for maximum-likelihood exponential fitting.

The finding across both misspecification attempts: per-cluster fitting beats a single
pooled rate by a large, significant margin under an easier-than-assumed model, an
exactly-matched model, and a genuinely adversarial one. Whether learned continues to track
oracle as closely as it does here under a still harder mixture, or with less calibration
data, is a natural next sweep this report does not run.

\subsection{Ablation: isolating the gate and the eviction term}
\label{sec:ablation}

I ran six configurations for 10 seeds each on the same workload: stock LRU with the gate
disabled; stock LFU with the gate disabled, the floor of comparison per Biton and
Friedman's own finding that LRU underperforms on semantic workloads; a documented
substitute for Biton and Friedman's released policy with the gate disabled; the validity
gate combined with plain LFU eviction; the validity gate combined with full FreCoS
eviction; and the validity gate combined with FreCoS eviction with the size-normalization
term removed.

Read as hit rate alone, the gate looks costly: 0.052 down to 0.017, a drop of about a
third. Read per query, useful-hit-rate reported per seed and then medianed (not derived
from the medians of hit-rate and stale-hit-rate, which would give a slightly different
number), the picture changes: 0.0170 at the floor to 0.0165 at the full stack, a drop of
about 3\%, while stale-hit-rate falls from 0.680 to 0.035, roughly 19-fold.
cost\_saved\_usd is unchanged across every row (0.44--0.45) for the same reason: it counts
only non-stale hits, and the set of queries that hit at all barely moves. The 19x figure in
stale-hit-rate is a real number, but the comparison it flatters, against LFU with no
freshness mechanism at all, is one any correctly-implemented TTL would win. The more
informative comparison in Section~\ref{sec:brackets} is learned against global pooling, a
4x reduction, since that isolates what \emph{learning} the rate buys over the cheaper
alternative of a hand-set global TTL; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to this point.

One row produced an unplanned but informative finding. The documented substitute for
Biton and Friedman's policy turned out identical to plain LFU in every correctness and cost
column, seed by seed, to full precision. This traces to a real workload property, not a
bug: at this configuration's roughly 5\% hit rate, almost every entry at eviction time has
never been accessed a second time, so the substitute's frequency-divided-by-cost score
evaluates to zero for essentially every candidate, and the policy falls through to LFU's
own tie-break. I did not have access to Biton and Friedman's released code in this build
environment, and the substitute is, on this workload, indistinguishable from the LFU floor
it was meant to sit alongside. This means the eviction axis in this report is compared only
against count-based policies, not against the literature's own strongest published policy,
which weakens the positioning claim in Section~1.2 that I compare against the literature
rather than the product baseline; the literature comparison there is qualitative, not
experimental.

The predicted interaction, that the gate carries most of the improvement with the decay
term contributing less on top than alone, held more strongly than I expected. Isolating the
gate's own effect (gate-plus-LFU against the floor) gives $\Delta = -0.638$
($U = 0.0$, $p \approx 0.0002$, $r = 1.0$); isolating FreCoS's decay term on top of an
already-active gate gives a further $\Delta = -0.007$, an order of magnitude smaller and
not statistically distinguishable from zero ($U = 50.5$, $p \approx 0.97$, $r = 0.01$). The
gate accounts for roughly 99\% of the total move from floor to full stack. I checked both
components for a shared cluster-identity bug regardless, since that was the specific
integration failure I expected to be most likely; neither disagreed with the other about
an entry's cluster.

I expected the decay term to contribute measurably less than it contributes alone, not
nothing measurable at all. The size-normalization row was likewise indistinguishable from
the full stack on every metric at this cache size. I reran the same size-term comparison at
a fifth of this cache size (330 entries, where eviction pressure is far higher) to check
whether the null result was an artifact of low eviction pressure at 1650 entries: it was
not ($U = 50.5$, $p \approx 0.97$, median stale-hit-rate 0.021 with size versus 0.021
without). With the gate already filtering out anything stale, the entries eviction chooses
among are uniformly fresh, and neither the decay term nor the size term differentiates much
among them, at either cache size I tested.

\subsection{Cache-size sweep and the correctness-efficiency trade-off}
\label{ssec:sweeps}

I swept cache size across 5 points spanning 5\%--80\% of the workload's distinct-answer
working set, full stack throughout. Hit rate rises from 0.013 (median 98 hits) at the
smallest point to 0.017 (132 hits) at 30\%, then flattens completely; the three largest
points produce identical per-seed hit counts. The knee, where marginal hit-rate gain per
additional entry drops by more than an order of magnitude relative to the previous step,
falls at 30\% of the working set: the slope drops roughly 13x into that point and is
exactly zero afterward. This cache size anchors every other sweep here.

The next sweep produces the trade-off curve this report's argument rests on most heavily:
holding cache size fixed at that knee, I sweep the gate's target confidence level.

The exchange is not uniform: most of the stale-hit-rate benefit is captured in the first
step, 0.80 to 0.90 (0.126 to 0.042), while hit rate erodes at a steadier pace across the
full range. The cheapest correctness gains come early; pushing toward 0.99 buys a shrinking
reduction in staleness for roughly the same ongoing hit-rate cost. This is a trade-off
curve, not evidence a stricter gate is uniformly better: an operator chooses a point on it
to suit their own tolerance for stale answers against misses.

A fourth sweep varied cluster count at 5, 10, 20, and 50. This came out non-monotone on
both metrics, with every adjacent confidence interval overlapping heavily, so no directional
effect is distinguishable from noise at 10 seeds per point. A plausible mechanism: more
clusters shrinks each one's calibration sample, making the learned rate noisier, while
finer-grained clusters could in principle track heterogeneous half-lives more precisely,
two effects pulling in opposite directions. This result sits in a supplementary table
(\texttt{analysis/figures/supplementary.csv}) rather than a headline figure, since it clears
neither the effect-size nor confidence-interval bar the rest of this report's figures were
held to.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Fitting a staleness rate from observed data beats both alternatives I tested, a single
pooled rate and no rate at all, by a large, significant margin, under an easier-than-fitted
model, a matched model, and a genuinely adversarial mixture model
(Section~\ref{ssec:misspec}). Under the matched and easier models, learned tracks oracle
almost exactly, narrower than the ``strictly between the two brackets'' shape I originally
expected: it means per-cluster maximum-likelihood estimation is accurate on this workload,
not that learning lands partway to a ceiling it approaches but cannot reach. Under the
adversarial mixture, a real, non-degenerate gap between learned and oracle finally opens,
though not one distinguishable from noise at $n=10$. I report each finding as it actually
came out rather than as I expected any of them to.

I expected the gate to carry most of the stale-hit-rate improvement with the decay term
contributing less on top than alone. What I found is that the decay term's contribution is
not distinguishable from zero at all, at either cache size I tested. The gate is the
correctness mechanism; the eviction term, as measured here, does not add detectable work
on top of it. A system wanting only the staleness-correctness gain could plausibly adopt
the gate alone. That gate is, at bottom, a time-to-live learned per cluster rather than set globally by
hand; Section~1.1 dismisses plain time-to-live caching as what weaker products already do,
and this project's strongest result is a better time-to-live, not a mechanism outside that
family. ``Better'' is measured directly, not asserted: Section~\ref{sec:brackets} is exactly
the comparison between this learned per-cluster time-to-live and a single global one,
learned's median stale-hit-rate 0.035 against global's 0.144, a significant 4x difference.
The global arm there is the hand-set time-to-live a system without this project's
per-cluster fitting would use instead.

The correctness-efficiency trade-off in Section~\ref{ssec:sweeps} most needs to be read as
a trade-off, not a win: raising the gate's target confidence buys a large stale-hit-rate
reduction at a small, real hit-rate cost, front-loaded so early gains are cheap and later
ones cost more. I do not claim a universally correct point on that curve.

Three limitations bound how far these results generalize. Every staleness result here
comes from a synthetic, self-authored workload; I attempted external ground truth from
Wikipedia and stopped after a feasibility spike measured 40\% rule agreement against an
80\% bar set in advance. The harness's exact-match index caps every absolute rate well
below a real semantic index and leaves false-hit-rate carrying no signal
(Section~\ref{ssec:simmodel}); every finding is a relative comparison across conditions
sharing that limitation, and I have not verified a semantic index would preserve the same
rankings. Neither prior-art comparator, Biton and Friedman's released policy nor Cortex's
LCFU, was actually run against this workload; the eviction axis is measured only against
count-based policies.

\section{Conclusion and future work}

I set out to name a gap in how semantic caches are evaluated, stale-hit-rate, and to build
a system that closes it. That system has two components, matching the two gaps named in
Section~1.1: a staleness-aware serve-path gate, and a cost-aware eviction term. The first
did the work I hoped for. The gate converts almost all stale hits into misses while
removing only about 3\% of useful hits, at under a microsecond of measured overhead per
decision, and the learned per-cluster rate that feeds it beats naive pooling significantly
and consistently across three tested half-life distributions, including a genuinely
adversarial one. The second did not: the eviction value function's cost and size terms
behave correctly under every property test I wrote, but their measured contribution on top
of an active gate is not distinguishable from zero at either cache size I tested. Of the
two gaps this project set out to close, one is closed and measured; the other's mechanism
is implemented and tested but has not been shown to add value on this workload.

A real semantic index in place of the exact-match harness would likely change every
absolute number here and is the change most likely to make results comparable to
production; I expect the relative findings to survive it, but have not verified that.
Running Biton and Friedman's policy directly, rather than the documented substitute,
would close the comparator gap in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. Calibrating the
generator's distributions against a real inference trace and content-update corpus would
strengthen external validity. Dynamic re-clustering under drift, a probabilistic staleness
budget in the style of FreshCache, and admission control on top of the current policy
remain future work, as in the original design.

\section*{Appendix A: correctness evidence}

Correctness is checked at three levels. A reference oracle
(\texttt{tests/oracle/reference\_cache.py}) implements the same hit/miss/stale decision as
the extension using a naive dict-and-linear-scan approach with no shared code, and every
pipeline decision is cross-checked against it in \texttt{tests/test\_stock\_parity.py}. A
callable invariant suite (\texttt{tests/invariants.py}) is run not only in unit tests but
inside \texttt{benchmarks/harness.py} on every experimental run, checking budget bounds,
argmin eviction selection, no entry served past its cluster's time-to-live, and that
staleness decisions derive from creation time, never last-access time. Property-based tests
in \texttt{tests/test\_frecos.py} check the eviction value function's monotonicity in each
of its four terms independently.

The most persuasive of these artifacts is a property test that failed against my original
design rather than a bug I found by inspection: \texttt{test\_cold\_start\_entry\_scores\_above\_zero}
in \texttt{tests/test\_frecos.py} asserts that a fresh entry must score above zero, and it
failed against the literal $\log(1+\text{freq})$ formula, which is how I found and fixed the
$\log(1+\text{freq}) \to \log(2+\text{freq})$ issue described in Section~3.1.
