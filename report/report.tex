\begin{abstract}
A semantic cache answers a new query by returning the stored response to a sufficiently
similar older one. Such caches are evaluated on hit rate, latency, and sometimes
false-hit rate. None of those asks whether a served hit was still \emph{true} at the
moment it was served. I name that quantity \emph{stale-hit-rate}, the fraction of served
hits whose content had already expired, and present FreCoS, an extension to GPTCache
that fits an exponential validity-decay rate per query cluster and consumes it twice: as
a hard validity gate on the serve path, and as a decay term in a cost-aware eviction
value function. Cluster identity is recovered by k-means over embeddings from the
cache's own encoder, so both consumers see only signals a deployed system would have. On
a seeded synthetic workload with per-cluster content half-lives, the gate cuts
stale-hit-rate roughly eightfold against a frequency-based floor, and fitting a rate per
cluster beats pooling across clusters with a large, significant effect while staying
measurably short of an oracle that reads the true rates. Under real eviction pressure the
cost term beats a pure-frequency policy on cost saved. False-hit-rate, which the semantic
index makes measurable and which is reported beside every result, stays near 0.90 and is
the dominant limit on absolute numbers here, though not on relative comparisons.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}

A semantic cache sits in front of an expensive backend, usually a large language model
call, and serves a stored response when an incoming query lands close enough in embedding
space to one already answered. The promise: skip the call, return the same answer faster
and for free. But ``the same answer'' is a claim about time as well as similarity, and
most semantic caches never check the time part.

This matters most where a language model sits between a user and an answer about content
that changes on its own schedule. Monitoring how a brand is represented across
AI-generated answers is one such case. The same style of question gets asked repeatedly,
exactly the workload a semantic cache absorbs, but the facts behind it do not sit still. A
cache that asks only ``have I seen a similar question'' and never ``is that answer still
true'' keeps serving a stale answer indefinitely once written, and there is no way to tell a
correct hit from a wrong one, because stale-hit-rate does not exist in standard cache
evaluation.

Two gaps follow, and each motivates one of the components I add. The serve path has no
notion of staleness: a cached answer about a fact with its own expiry schedule is served
indefinitely, and ``found in cache'' is silently treated as ``correct.'' Eviction has no
cost term: count-based policies such as LRU and LFU treat two entries with
identical access patterns alike even when one is far more expensive to regenerate, so a
rarely accessed but costly entry can be evicted ahead of a cheap one accessed once more
recently.

Industry and research reflect this gap unevenly. Frontier model providers do exact-prefix
key-value caching with a wall-clock TTL and no similarity matching. Semantic-caching
products use a single fixed global threshold with plain TTL or count-based eviction, with
no notion of per-topic staleness. The research literature is further ahead, proposing
cost-aware and staleness-aware eviction formulas, but none names or measures
stale-hit-rate. I compare against that literature, not the weaker product baseline.

This report makes three contributions. The first is stale-hit-rate as a named, measured
metric, evaluated on a harness that uses a real cosine-similarity index and real k-means
clustering rather than exact-match lookup and ground-truth cluster labels, so the gate
and the eviction term see only what a deployed cache would see. The second is evidence
that per-cluster staleness fitting does measurable work: learned rates beat a pooled rate
with a large, significant effect, and stay significantly short of an oracle that reads the
generator's true rates. That is the pattern a fitter working off an imperfect but real
signal should produce. The third is a cost-aware eviction result measured under real
eviction pressure, where FreCoS's cost term beats a pure-frequency floor with a
large effect, while an insertion-order baseline beats both for a reason
Section~\ref{ssec:costaware} traces to the workload rather than to any staleness
mechanism.

\subsection{Related work}

The closest prior art, and my primary eviction comparator, is Biton and Friedman's work
on semantic-cache eviction~\cite{bitonfriedman2026}. It proves optimal offline
semantic-cache eviction is NP-hard, supplies polynomial-time heuristics, and presents
online policies combining recency, frequency, and locality, evaluated on GPTCache with
released code, part of why I chose GPTCache here. Two of their findings shape my
design: LRU is weak on semantic workloads, and frequency-based policies are a stronger
baseline, which is why every comparison in this report treats an LFU-class policy, not
LRU, as the floor. FreCoS differs in adding two orthogonal signals, regeneration cost and
content validity over time, and in evaluating against a correctness metric their setting
never defines.

The structurally closest published formula is Cortex's LCFU policy~\cite{cortex2026},
scoring an entry as log-scaled frequency times cost times latency times a staleness term,
divided by size, with a TTL purge on top. The difference is narrow but load-bearing:
Cortex's staleness term is a static score assigned by a language model at write time,
while FreCoS learns a decay rate per cluster from observed staleness.
Section~\ref{sec:brackets} tests whether that learning does measurable work. I did not
reimplement Cortex's formula, since it relies on remote tool-call latency metadata with
no equivalent here.

Several other lines touch individual pieces without matching the combination:
GDSF~\cite{gdsf2001} ages access recency, not content validity; Category-Aware
caching~\cite{categoryaware2025} assigns load-based, not learned, per-category TTLs (which
is why I cut a third planned component, calibrated per-cluster thresholds, as a lossy
special case of it); FreshCache~\cite{freshcache2026} puts staleness in the serve path as
a probabilistic risk budget rather than a hard gate; SCALM~\cite{scalm2024} clusters
entries much as my k-means step does but with no staleness-aware significance score;
MeanCache~\cite{meancache2024} is cost-motivated but does not target eviction;
W-TinyLFU~\cite{tinylfu2015} is admission control, orthogonal to both my contributions. I
considered and rejected vCache~\cite{vcache2025}: its contribution, per-prompt threshold
learning with eviction explicitly out of focus, would have diluted rather than sharpened
the comparison.

\section{Baseline}

\subsection{Why GPTCache}

I extended GPTCache~\cite{gptcache} rather than a more actively maintained semantic cache,
for four reasons. First, its most recent release (v0.1.44, August 2024) is frozen, since the
maintainers state new integrations have stopped. That makes a better scientific control
than a moving target: the baseline measured early in this project is byte-identical to the
one measured at the end. Second, architectural fit, verified by reading the vendored
source directly: every component I touch sits behind a named interface, so my extension is
purely additive (a metadata field via composition, a serving-path gate in one shared
decision helper). FreCoS's eviction policy is the one exception, added by monkeypatching
\texttt{EvictionBase.get()} rather than through an extension hook, because that factory
has none: it is a hardcoded if/elif chain with no lookup table a caller could add to.
That path is verified end to end against a real
\texttt{gptcache.manager.data\_manager.SSDataManager} in
\texttt{tests/test\_gptcache\_integration.py}. I never edit vendored code. Third, GPTCache
is the baseline the closest prior art already uses, letting me in principle run Biton and
Friedman's released policy directly, though in practice I substituted a documented proxy
(Section~\ref{sec:ablation}). Fourth, its default embedding backend runs
CPU-deterministically, keeping every run reproducible without a GPU. One trade made
deliberately: GPTCache's README states new contributions are not merged, foreclosing this
course's upstream-adoption bonus.

\subsection{What GPTCache does today}

GPTCache serves a cached response when a query is semantically close enough to one already
seen, using a single global cosine threshold (0.8 by default). Its eviction policies are
purely count-based (LRU, LFU, FIFO, random); none reads generation time or regeneration
cost, and none retains metadata on an evicted entry. Creation and last-access timestamps
\emph{are} persisted, with no downstream consumer. That is the gap my extension
repurposes. There is no admission control, and no cost, false-hit, or staleness tracking
anywhere in the built-in reporting layer.

\section{Design}

\subsection{One learned artifact, two consumers}

FreCoS fits a per-cluster staleness model and uses it at both ends of the cache's
lifecycle: as a hard validity gate when serving a candidate hit, and as a soft decay term
when choosing an eviction victim. The metric that makes both uses visible is
stale-hit-rate: the fraction of served hits whose content was already invalid when served.

\paragraph{Clustering.} Cluster assignment comes from a fixed, seeded k-means over
cached-query embeddings from GPTCache's default ONNX model, fit once per trace on the
calibration split, with every row assigned to its nearest centroid. Nothing downstream
ever reads the generator's true label; it is recorded only to compute the adjusted Rand
index against the learned assignment (\texttt{cluster\_ari}, reported with every
bracket-style result). Both consumers read the same, possibly wrong, cluster identity and
neither recomputes it independently. I checked the ablation directly for disagreement
between them and found none.

\paragraph{Fitting.} For each cluster, a decay rate $\lambda_c$ is fit from observed
staleness in a held-out calibration split, assuming exponential validity decay:
$\Pr[\text{still valid after } \Delta t] = e^{-\lambda_c \Delta t}$. A TTL follows at
confidence level $c$ as $\text{ttl} = -\ln(c) / \lambda_c$. Three modes produce this table
with the same output shape: \emph{learned} fits each cluster independently by maximum
likelihood, falling back to a pooled rate below thirty observations; \emph{global} pools
every cluster regardless of sample size; \emph{oracle} takes the rate from the generator's
ground truth, an upper bound never available in a real deployment.

\paragraph{Consumer 1: the validity gate.} On a candidate hit, if the entry's age exceeds
its cluster's TTL, it is treated as a miss and the event counts as a stale hit
\emph{prevented} rather than served. Age is measured from creation time, never last-access
time: staleness is a property of when an answer was generated, not of how recently it was
asked for, so a popular but outdated entry is not treated as fresh just because it is
popular.

\paragraph{Consumer 2: the eviction value function.}

\begin{equation}
\text{value} = \log(2 + \text{freq}) \cdot \log(1 + \kappa \cdot \text{regen\_cost}) \cdot e^{-\lambda_c \cdot \text{age}}
\label{eq:value}
\end{equation}

Here $\text{age}$ is again from creation time; $\kappa = 1000$ log-compresses regeneration
cost so its heavy tail cannot dominate; the victim is the lowest-value entry, ties broken
by oldest creation then lowest entry id. Unlike GDSF-style formulas and Cortex's LCFU,
there is no division by \texttt{size\_bytes}. Eviction here runs under an entry-count
budget, not a byte budget: freeing a 10-byte entry and a 10{,}000-byte entry both free
exactly one slot, so size-normalization has no budget economics to act through. An
isolation run at the highest-eviction-pressure cache size in the sweep confirmed this
empirically: with and without the term, FreCoS was statistically indistinguishable on
every metric (\texttt{results/ablation/size\_term\_isolation/}). Byte-budgeted eviction,
where size would carry real meaning, is future work.

The frequency term is $\log(2+\text{freq})$ rather than the more natural
$\log(1+\text{freq})$, and the difference matters more than it looks:
$\log(1+\text{freq})$ is exactly zero for a never-accessed entry, which zeroes the whole
product regardless of cost or freshness and makes a freshly written entry the next victim
every time. A property test caught this against the literal formula (Appendix~A);
$\log(2+\text{freq})$ has the same concave shape but is strictly positive at zero
frequency.

Because the gate already refreshes anything past its TTL, the decay term is not doing
staleness prevention on its own. It acts as a soft prior among candidates the gate has
already judged fresh. Section~\ref{sec:ablation} tests exactly that.

\section{Experimental setup}

\subsection{Workload}

All experiments run on W1, a deterministic, seeded synthetic workload generator built for
this project. Before other implementation work I timeboxed a feasibility spike into a
second workload derived from Wikipedia revision history; an automatic sentence-change rule
agreed with hand labels only 40\% of the time against an 80\% go/no-go bar set in advance
(\texttt{docs/w2-feasibility.md}), so I proceeded with W1 alone.

W1 generates queries across several streams inside each topic cluster: canonical queries
establishing a ground-truth answer, near-duplicate paraphrases, novel long-tail queries
that never repeat, and time-shifted repeats. Every cluster draws its own half-life and
regeneration-cost distribution, and at least one repeat per answer lands past its true
expiry, giving the gate something to reject. Each trace splits by arrival time, 30\%
calibration and 70\% evaluation, so no parameter is fit on data it is later scored
against.

One workload-design requirement is easy to get wrong, and getting it wrong silently
invalidates everything downstream: \emph{cluster identity has to be recoverable from query
text alone}, or per-cluster fitting is never actually exercised. A template that varies
only in embedded integers across clusters is not recoverable, because a general-purpose
embedder scores such text at 0.6--0.9 cosine similarity regardless of true cluster, which
puts adjusted Rand index at random-assignment level (0.02--0.06). W1's canonical text
therefore crosses a distinct topic phrase per cluster (51 real-world subjects, e.g.\ ``the
Roman Empire'', ``quantum computing'') with a distinct aspect and qualifier phrase per
answer within that cluster (750 combinations). That puts median \texttt{cluster\_ari} at
0.42, a substantial signal though not a perfect one. \texttt{tests/test\_w1.py} asserts it
stays above 0.5 on a held-out check.

The default generator draws half-life from an exponential distribution, matching the
fitter's own assumption exactly. Section~\ref{ssec:misspec} reruns the bracketing
experiment with that assumption deliberately violated: once with half-life drawn from a
Weibull distribution (same mean, different shape), and once, the stronger check, from a
mixture of two exponentials within each cluster.

The generator's two ground-truth distributions, regeneration cost and content half-life,
were meant to be calibrated against the Azure LLM Inference Trace and observed
update-frequency in a public corpus. Neither was reachable from my development
environment, so both are parameterized from commonly cited public figures instead: a
plausibility check, not a real external fit, and a limit on external validity.

\subsection{Harness and simulation model}
\label{ssec:simmodel}

The harness is a pure trace replayer; no language model is ever called. On a miss,
response content and cost are whatever the trace recorded, and miss latency is drawn from
a seeded log-normal scaled by entry size, deterministic per query. That distribution is a
documented placeholder, not fit to a real trace. Hit latency and extension overhead (index lookup
plus gate check) are measured for real: 0.348\,ms per decision
(Table~\ref{tab:latency}), dominated by the cosine search rather than the gate. Reported
latency is therefore a hybrid of measured and simulated cost: the right trade for keeping
runs free and deterministic, but a real limitation.

Lookup uses \texttt{benchmarks.semantic\_index.SemanticIndex}, brute-force cosine
similarity at GPTCache's default 0.8 threshold. Having a real index rather than
exact-match lookup is what makes false-hit-rate a live metric, and it is high: median
0.90--0.92 across every bracket-style experiment. Direct inspection of embeddings shows
why. Two different aspects of the \emph{same} topic (two distinct questions about
Renaissance art, say) score 0.7--0.8 cosine similarity, close enough to the match
threshold to cross it, because to a general-purpose embedder they are related text. This is a recognizable failure mode for a real semantic cache rather than a
workload artifact, but it does mean every hit-rate number below should be read alongside
its false-hit-rate.

One simplification in the harness's index changes what a baseline label means: the index
does not update \texttt{last\_access} when an entry is
hit, so the LRU baseline in fact evicts in insertion order. I report that row as
insertion-order eviction throughout; true LRU is untested here, and
Section~\ref{ssec:costaware} reads its result accordingly.

The cache starts empty in every run, and the first 10\% of the evaluation split warms it
and is excluded from every metric.

\subsection{Metrics and statistics}
\label{ssec:stats}

Stale-hit-rate is the fraction of served hits where serve time exceeds the entry's true
expiry. False-hit-rate is the fraction where the served answer's identity does not match
the query's. Useful-hit-rate is the fraction of served hits (not of all scored
queries) that are both correct and non-stale. It is counted directly by a per-row predicate
(\texttt{benchmarks.metrics.is\_useful\_hit}) rather than reconstructed as
$(n_\text{hits} - n_\text{stale} - n_\text{false}) / n_\text{hits}$, because
\texttt{is\_stale\_hit} and \texttt{is\_false\_hit} are independent predicates that can
both hold of the same hit: the subtraction double-counts the overlap and goes negative
once both rates are large.

Cost saved sums regeneration cost over non-stale hits only, and does \emph{not} also
exclude false hits. At this workload's false-hit-rate the reported cost-saved numbers are
therefore dominated by hits that returned the wrong answer, and
\texttt{cost\_saved\_usd} should be read as an upper bound, not as money saved by correct
answers. Adjusted Rand index (\texttt{cluster\_ari}) measures how well the learned
assignment matches the true labels: 1.0 is a perfect match, 0.0 is what random assignment
gives in expectation.

Two primary comparisons are designated up front and reported without correction: learned
versus global (does per-cluster learning beat pooling), and gate-on versus the LFU floor
(does the gate do measurable work). Every other Mann-Whitney test is secondary, corrected
via Holm-Bonferroni across the secondary family, with raw and adjusted $p$-values reported
together (\texttt{analysis/\allowbreak multiple\_comparisons.py}).

Every configuration ran five independent seeds, each with its own freshly generated
3{,}000-query trace. Results are medians with 95\% CIs from a percentile bootstrap over
the five per-seed values (10{,}000 resamples, fixed seed), with raw hit and stale-hit
counts alongside every rate, since a five-seed bootstrap CI is coarser than it looks.
Group comparisons use Mann-Whitney U with rank-biserial correlation, $r = 2 U_a/(n_1 n_2)
- 1$, as effect size (\texttt{analysis/stats.py}, hand-rolled and unit-tested against
known closed-form cases: complete separation gives $r=\pm1$, identical samples give
$r=0$). At $n=5$ the smallest achievable two-sided $p$ is about 0.008 and cannot alone
distinguish a large effect from a small one, so effect sizes accompany every $p$-value. A
non-significant result is an absence of detected difference, never equivalence; where I
claim two conditions behave alike, raw counts back it up rather than a large $p$-value.

Every experiment ran on the machine recorded in its own \texttt{env.json}: an Apple M2 Pro
(10 cores), 16\,GB RAM, macOS, Python 3.13. It is a shared development machine, not a
dedicated benchmarking host, which matters for the peak-RSS figure this report already
flags as unreliable.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Bracketing: does learned staleness beat naive pooling?}
\label{sec:brackets}

This experiment tests the core claim behind fitting a rate per cluster at all: does it
beat a rate pooled across every cluster, and how far is it from the true rate a real
system could never observe? I ran three lambda sources (global, learned, oracle) for
5 seeds each on a 3{,}000-query W1 configuration, gate enabled at $c=0.90$, FreCoS
eviction throughout. Cluster identity comes from real k-means over query embeddings; the
oracle arm alone still reads the true label internally, since its lambda table is keyed by
true cluster identity and has no other meaning once clustering is imperfect.

equally to all three arms, so it does not confound the comparison between them.

Three follow-ups (a scarcer-calibration bracket and the two misspecification checks
below) reproduce the same qualitative pattern, with \texttt{cluster\_ari} in the same
0.40--0.45 range. That rules out calibration sample size and half-life-distribution shape
as confounds for the clustering-quality finding.

\subsection{Ablation: isolating the gate from the eviction term}
\label{sec:ablation}

policies (insertion-order, LFU, and a documented substitute for Biton and Friedman's
released policy), then the validity gate combined with plain LFU and with full FreCoS
eviction. LFU, not insertion-order, is the floor of comparison, following Biton and
Friedman's finding that LRU-class policies underperform on semantic workloads.

feeding it is right, because the same assignment is used consistently at calibration and
at serve time.

The two ties have different causes, and only one of them is interesting. Rows 1--3 tie
because at this configuration's real semantic hit rate ($\sim$89\%) only 178--210 misses
occur out of 1890 scored queries, under a 412-entry budget. Eviction therefore never runs
at all, and the three policies tie because none is ever called to select a victim, not
because they agree on one. Section~\ref{ssec:costaware} confirms this by rerunning the
same gate-off configuration at a cache size small enough to force real eviction, where
they do differ. Rows 4--5 tie for a substantive reason: FreCoS's decay term on top of an
already-active gate gives $\Delta = 0.000$, byte-identical. The gate accounts for the
entire measurable stale-hit-rate improvement, matching the design's own framing of the
decay term as a soft prior: with the gate active, every entry eviction sees is already
known-fresh, so the decay term has nothing left to differentiate on.

\subsection{A fair test of cost-aware eviction}
\label{ssec:costaware}

Every ablation row above ran either with the gate on, which pre-filters stale entries
before eviction ever sees them, or at a cache size where eviction never fires. Neither
gives FreCoS's cost and decay terms anything to differentiate on. This experiment turns
the gate off and runs three eviction policies for 5 seeds each at a 25-entry cache, scaled
down from the 412 entries the ablation uses in proportion to the smaller trace so that the
cache reliably fills. It does: over 1{,}400 misses occur per run out of 1890 scored
queries, against 178--210 in the gate-off ablation rows. That is real eviction pressure.

FreCoS versus LFU on cost saved: $U_a=21.0$, $p\approx0.076$, $r\approx0.68$. FreCoS is

FreCoS's cost term shows real, directionally consistent work, and it is the result the
gate-on ablation structurally could not surface.

Insertion-order eviction, however, beats FreCoS on cost saved ($U_a=7.0$, $p\approx0.251$,
$r\approx-0.44$, medium effect) and is strictly lower on stale-hit-rate ($U_a=25.0$,
$p\approx0.0090$, $r\approx1.00$, perfect separation). The mechanism has nothing to do with staleness
modeling: with no gate active, evicting the oldest-created entry is very nearly a
definition of evicting the entry most likely to have expired. Insertion-order eviction
gets that freshness benefit for free, without a staleness model, a calibration split or a
gate, which challenges this project's premise on any workload where write age and eviction
order line up this well. What holds
up is narrower than the premise but real: FreCoS beats the pure-frequency floor on cost
saved with a large effect. It does not beat insertion order, and this report does not
claim otherwise. Whether true LRU (which this harness does not exercise,
Section~\ref{ssec:simmodel}) would show the same advantage is untested, and worth testing
because Biton and Friedman report the opposite for LRU on semantic workloads.

\subsection{Cache size and the correctness-efficiency trade-off}

the figure and its caption cannot drift apart from the CSV. This cache size anchors the
remaining sweeps.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Three findings hold, at very different strengths.

The gate is the strongest of them. It cuts stale-hit-rate roughly 8-fold against the LFU floor
with perfect separation across seeds, and it does so \emph{independently of clustering
quality}, because the same cluster assignment is used at calibration and at serve time: a
wrong but consistent cluster still yields a usable TTL. That robustness is why this
component survives every variation tested here.

Per-cluster learning is the more interesting finding. Learned rates beat a pooled rate
with a large, significant effect and stay significantly short of oracle, which is what a
working fitter should produce given an imperfect signal (median \texttt{cluster\_ari}
0.42). The result also depends on a workload property that is easy to get wrong: if
cluster identity is not recoverable from query text, per-cluster fitting is never
exercised at all, and a suite can report a clean-looking null result that measures nothing
(Section~\ref{ssec:simmodel}). Any future work extending this to a real corpus should
verify \texttt{cluster\_ari} before trusting any per-cluster result.

Cost-aware eviction is the weakest. FreCoS's cost term does beat the pure-frequency floor
with a large effect under real eviction pressure, which counts as a positive result. But
insertion-order eviction beats FreCoS on both metrics tested, for a reason that has
nothing to do with modeling staleness and everything to do with write age correlating with
eviction order on this trace. On workloads with that structure, a policy with no staleness
model at all is hard to beat.

Two limitations cut across everything above. First, false-hit-rate near 0.90: the semantic
index matches same-topic, different-aspect queries at 0.7--0.8 cosine similarity, close
enough to the 0.8 threshold to cross it. This applies uniformly across arms, so relative
comparisons stand, but no absolute number here should be read as production-realistic.
Second, circularity: stale-hit-rate is scored against \texttt{valid\_until}, the same
ground-truth field that supplies the oracle arm's lambda. The metric, the ground truth it
is checked against, and the ceiling one arm is measured relative to all come from one
self-authored generator. Relative comparisons within an experiment remain valid, since
both sides are scored against the same ground truth, but no number here is a claim
about staleness detection against an independent notion of correctness. My attempt at
external ground truth from Wikipedia stopped at a feasibility spike that measured 40\%
rule agreement against an 80\% bar. Neither prior-art comparator, Biton and Friedman's
released policy nor Cortex's LCFU, was run against this workload, so the eviction axis is
measured only against count-based policies and FreCoS itself.

Finally, scale: 3{,}000 queries and 5 seeds per configuration, chosen because embedding
dominates runtime and the conclusions here do not depend on trace size. The cost is
statistical power: several comparisons show a large effect and a consistent direction
without clearing $p<0.05$ at $n=5$ (FreCoS vs.\ LFU on cost saved, most notably). Every
such case is reported with its effect size next to the non-significant $p$-value rather
than as a null result.

\section{Conclusion and future work}

I set out to name a gap in how semantic caches are evaluated, stale-hit-rate, and to
build a system that closes it, with two components matching the two gaps in
Section~1.1. The validity gate does the job: an 8-fold cut in stale-hit-rate with perfect
separation, robust to clustering error. The per-cluster learning that sharpens it holds up
as well, beating pooling with a large, significant effect while staying short of oracle. The
eviction term's cost-awareness beats a pure-frequency floor once tested under real
eviction pressure, but loses to insertion-order eviction on this trace for reasons
unrelated to staleness modeling.

The most consequential next step is the false-hit-rate gap: same-topic,
different-aspect queries still land close to the index's match threshold, so a more
semantically distant aspect vocabulary, or a real corpus in place of a synthetic template,
would likely reduce false-hit-rate without touching the clustering pipeline.
Excluding false hits from \texttt{cost\_saved\_usd} is a prepared, straightforward metric
change, deferred here only because it would invalidate every committed
\texttt{results.csv}; the metric carries an explicit caveat in the meantime. Making the
harness update \texttt{last\_access} on a hit would let the same suite test true LRU
rather than insertion order, which matters given how strong the insertion-order baseline
turned out to be. Beyond that: running Biton and Friedman's policy and Cortex's LCFU
directly, now that a real index exists to run them against; calibrating the generator
against a real inference trace; and breaking the circularity between stale-hit-rate and
its generator-supplied ground truth, which would be the single strongest improvement
available. Dynamic re-clustering, a FreshCache-style probabilistic staleness budget,
byte-budgeted eviction (where the size term absent from Equation~\ref{eq:value} would
carry real meaning), and admission control remain future work.

\section*{Appendix A: correctness evidence}

Correctness is checked at four levels. A reference oracle
(\texttt{tests/oracle/reference\_cache.py}) implements the same hit/miss/stale decision as
the extension using a naive dict-and-linear-scan approach with no shared code, and every
pipeline decision is cross-checked against it in \texttt{tests/test\_stock\_parity.py}. A
callable invariant suite (\texttt{tests/invariants.py}) runs inside both the unit tests and
\texttt{benchmarks/harness.py} on every experimental run, checking budget bounds, argmin
eviction selection, that no entry is served past its cluster's TTL, and that staleness
decisions derive from creation time and never last-access time. Property-based tests
(\texttt{tests/test\_frecos.py}) check that the eviction value function is monotonic in
each term independently. Finally, \texttt{tests/test\_gptcache\_integration.py} drives a
real \texttt{gptcache.manager.data\_manager.SSDataManager} through enough writes to force
eviction and confirms the victim GPTCache's own eviction factory selects matches what
\texttt{FreCoSEviction.select\_victim()} computes from the same metadata. FreCoS is therefore
demonstrably reachable through GPTCache's real eviction path, not merely correct in
isolation.

The most persuasive of these artifacts is a property test that failed against my original
design rather than a bug found by inspection:
\texttt{test\_cold\_start\_entry\_scores\_above\_zero} asserts a fresh entry must score
above zero, and failed against the literal $\log(1+\text{freq})$ formula. That is how the
$\log(1+\text{freq}) \to \log(2+\text{freq})$ correction in Section~3.1 was found.
